SDWA — Safe Drinking Water Act 4 cards
PFAS National Primary Drinking Water Regulation (NPDWR)
Final rule in effect · revisions proposed · judicial review pending
EPA Office of Water · 40 CFR Parts 141 & 142 · announced Apr 10, 2024 · published Apr 26, 2024 · effective Jun 25, 2024
The 2024 NPDWR establishes MCLs of 4.0 ppt for PFOA and PFOS and 10 ppt for PFHxS, PFNA, and HFPO-DA, plus a Hazard Index of 1 for mixtures containing two or more of PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and PFBS (PFBS has no individual MCL). The rule remains in effect while EPA considers two proposed revisions published May 20, 2026; both comment periods closed July 20, 2026 following a July 7 virtual hearing. Source: Federal Register, 89 FR 32532.
  • FR Doc 2026-10086 / 91 FR 29425 (docket EPA-HQ-OW-2025-1742) — PFOA/PFOS compliance extension. EPA proposes a federal exemption framework (SDWA §§ 1416(f), 1450(a)(1)) letting eligible systems request additional time, through April 26, 2031, to comply with the PFOA/PFOS MCLs. Would not change the 4.0 ppt MCLs or existing monitoring/reporting requirements. Systems granted an exemption with any PFOA or PFOS result ≥12 ppt would be required to implement at least two interim control measures during the exemption period.
  • FR Doc 2026-10085 / 91 FR 29413 (docket EPA-HQ-OW-2025-0654) — Index PFAS rescission. EPA proposes rescinding the regulatory determinations and associated provisions for PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and Hazard Index mixtures of these substances plus PFBS. EPA estimates roughly $11.6M/year in quantified cost savings and $6.7M/year in quantified forgone benefits — figures specific to the PFHxS increment EPA could quantify, not a comprehensive estimate for all four Index PFAS; the proposal separately discusses additional, more uncertain and unquantified effects for PFNA, HFPO-DA, and PFBS.

A coalition of 19 state attorneys general (led by NC AG Jeff Jackson, filed July 21, 2026) and the D.C. attorney general submitted comments opposing the rescission, raising SDWA anti-backsliding and APA arguments. Separately, 113 House members signed a June 29 letter (released July 1) asking EPA to reconsider both proposals. Neither proposed change is final; the proposals remain under EPA review following comment-period closure. Source: NC Dept. of Justice press release.

Judicial review: the consolidated D.C. Circuit challenge to the 2024 rule (AWWA v. EPA) remains pending, with oral argument scheduled for September 18, 2026. EPA no longer defends the Index PFAS provisions (aligning with petitioners seeking their vacatur) but continues defending the PFOA/PFOS standards. The parties have jointly proposed splitting argument into two parts reflecting that split position; that format has not yet been confirmed by court order.

Implementation support: EPA's PFAS OUT initiative aims to engage roughly 3,000 water systems, contacting those above 4 ppt directly, with outreach expected to begin summer 2026. It connects systems to funding/technical-assistance information but does not itself provide funding or assistance. Source: EPA SDWA PFAS page.

UCMR 5 — Occurrence Data
Monitoring complete · final dataset released Aug 2026
EPA Office of Water · SDWA · 40 CFR Part 141
The Fifth Unregulated Contaminant Monitoring Rule (UCMR 5) required nationwide occurrence monitoring for 29 PFAS and lithium across public water systems from January 2023 through December 2025. EPA released the final UCMR 5 dataset in August 2026; EPA's Data Finder and downloadable occurrence-data files now contain the completed monitoring results. The dataset can be filtered by public water system, state, contaminant, source-water type, minimum reporting level, and other fields. Source: EPA UCMR 5 Data Finder.

UCMR 5 data characterize national occurrence and concentrations and support EPA outreach and technical-assistance activities. For PFAS subject to the drinking-water regulation, UCMR 5 results do not by themselves determine MCL compliance or noncompliance; regulatory compliance is determined through the applicable compliance-monitoring framework. Qualifying UCMR 5 data may, however, be usable for some initial-monitoring purposes under the NPDWR.

UCMR 6 — Proposed Monitoring for Ultrashort Organofluorine Compounds
Proposed · comments due Aug 31, 2026
EPA Office of Water · SDWA § 1445(a)(2) · 40 CFR Part 141 · proposed July 1, 2026
EPA's proposed Sixth Unregulated Contaminant Monitoring Rule (91 FR 39952; FR Doc. 2026-13263) would require nationwide occurrence monitoring for 30 unregulated contaminants during a 12-month period between January 2028 and December 2030. All CWSs/NTNCWSs serving >10,000 people would monitor; monitoring by systems serving 3,300–10,000 and a representative sample of 800 systems serving <3,300 is subject to available appropriations and sufficient laboratory capacity.

The proposal includes seven ultrashort organofluorine compounds, all analyzed by EPA Method 563. EPA identifies four as PFAS under the CCL 5 structural definition — PFPrS, PFPrA, PFEtS, and PFMOAA. TFA, TFMS, and TFSI do not meet that definition, but EPA proposes monitoring them because they are persistent, highly mobile, widely used in industry, and have health-effects information, and because Method 563 can capture them. The remaining contaminants are three pesticide metabolites, 13 semivolatile organic compounds, and seven purgeable organic compounds, analyzed using EPA Methods 540, 525.3, and 524.3 Enhanced Sensitivity.

EPA held proposal webinars August 11 and 12, 2026. Comments are due August 31, 2026 (docket EPA-HQ-OW-2023-0469). Large PWSs (>10,000 people) bear their own analytical costs; EPA covers costs for small PWSs, subject to appropriations and laboratory capacity. EPA did not propose microplastics for UCMR 6 — despite a November 2025 petition from the governors of seven states (CT, DE, IL, MD, MI, NJ, WI) and a related environmental-group petition — citing the absence of a validated national drinking-water analytical method; microplastics were instead added to the draft CCL 6 (see CCL 6 card) as a research/future-monitoring track. Pharmaceuticals were similarly considered but not proposed for UCMR 6; EPA points to the draft CCL 6 pharmaceuticals group as the relevant forward path.

CCL 6 — PFAS group
Comment period closed · SAB draft advice reviewed Aug 26
EPA Office of Water · SDWA § 1412(b)(1)(B)(i)
EPA's draft CCL 6 (Federal Register, April 6, 2026) proposes PFAS as one of four chemical groups, together with microplastics, pharmaceuticals, and disinfection byproducts, for the Contaminant Candidate List under SDWA. CCL listing imposes no requirements on regulated entities; it is a research and regulatory-prioritization step, not a proposed drinking-water standard. The public-comment period closed June 5, 2026 (docket EPA-HQ-OW-2022-0946), and final CCL 6 is expected to be signed by November 17, 2026.

SAB review, Aug. 26, 2026: EPA's Science Advisory Board CCL 6 Drinking Water Committee reviewed a draft advisory report that raises questions about the clarity and scope of both the PFAS and microplastics group definitions. For PFAS, the draft asks EPA to explain whether exclusion of compounds such as TFA is intentional and to consider individual listings where documented drinking-water occurrence may warrant evaluation. For microplastics, the draft asks EPA to clarify how chemicals occurring with or leaching from particles are treated so that relevant chemicals do not fall between group and individual-contaminant pathways. The committee draft is not final SAB consensus advice and does not represent EPA policy.

EPA's draft CCL 6 carries forward the same three-structure PFAS definition used for CCL 5; EPA has said that definition is specific to this CCL process rather than an agency-wide PFAS definition. The parallel proposed UCMR 6 would monitor seven ultrashort organofluorines, including substances that do not all meet the CCL structural definition, reinforcing the need to distinguish monitoring scope from PFAS classification.

Sources: EPA Draft CCL 6 · SAB committee draft report.

Rulemakings and studies underway
EPA Office of Water · Clean Water Act ELG program · 40 CFR Parts 414, 433, 445 & 437
EPA is evaluating and developing PFAS controls under the Clean Water Act effluent guidelines and pretreatment standards program across several industrial categories, at varying stages. No PFAS-specific ELG final rule has yet been promulgated for any of the sectors below.

Source: EPA OCPSF Effluent Guidelines.
  • Metal Finishing — chromium electroplating (40 CFR Part 433) — EPA is developing revisions addressing PFAS discharges specifically from chromium electroplating/chrome-finishing facilities (not metal finishing generally), continuing to review PFAS study and wastewater sampling data (docket EPA-HQ-OW-2022-0869). Current Unified Agenda targets an NPRM for February 2027. Source: EPA Metal Finishing Effluent Guidelines.
  • Landfills (40 CFR Part 445) — EPA has completed its detailed study and determined that revisions to the existing Landfills ELGs and pretreatment standards are warranted to address PFAS in landfill leachate. Current Unified Agenda targets an NPRM for July 2027. Source: EPA Landfills Effluent Guidelines.
  • Centralized Waste Treatment (40 CFR Part 437) — EPA is conducting a detailed study to characterize industrial PFAS sources and discharges from CWT facilities; no PFAS ELG rulemaking has been announced for this category yet. Source: EPA CWT Effluent Guidelines.

CWA PFAS Analytical Methods — Methods 1633A & 1621
Proposed for Part 136 · final action pending
EPA Office of Water · CWA § 304(h) · 40 CFR Part 136 · Docket EPA-HQ-OW-2024-0328
EPA's Methods Update Rule 22 (MUR 22), proposed January 21, 2025 (90 FR 6967; FR Doc. 2024-29239), would add EPA Method 1633A and EPA Method 1621 to the Clean Water Act analytical methods approved at 40 CFR Part 136. The comment period, originally set to close February 20, 2025, was reopened and extended to March 24, 2025 (90 FR 10043). EPA's current Unified Agenda lists MUR 22 at the Final Rule Stage and had projected final action for July 2026; no final rule has been published as of this refresh, and EPA states it is "in the process of evaluating the public comments as it develops the final rule." Sources: Federal Register, 90 FR 6967 · Unified Agenda, RIN 2040-AG37.

Method 1633A is a targeted LC-MS/MS method for 40 PFAS in wastewater, surface water, groundwater, soil, biosolids, sediment, landfill leachate, and fish tissue. It is not yet nationally required for CWA compliance monitoring, but EPA recommends it now for use in individual NPDES permits, and recommends Method 1633 or 1633A for PFAS monitoring in the NPDES and National Pretreatment Programs where permit authorities specify an appropriate method.

Method 1621 measures adsorbable organic fluorine (AOF) in aqueous samples using activated-carbon adsorption and combustion ion chromatography. It is a method-defined aggregate screening measure for organofluorines and can indicate fluorinated compounds not captured by targeted PFAS analysis, but it does not identify which individual PFAS or other organofluorines are present, and EPA cautions it does not quantify all captured organofluorines with equal accuracy and has known interferences. EPA describes it as complementary to targeted analysis such as Method 1633A; it also remains unapproved nationally under Part 136 while MUR 22 is pending. Source: EPA CWA Analytical Methods for PFAS.

Federal Biosolids — PFOA/PFOS Draft Guidance & Risk Assessment Review
Draft guidance · comments open through Oct 5, 2026
EPA Office of Water · CWA § 405 · Docket EPA-HQ-OW-2026-2509
EPA published a July 6, 2026 Federal Register notice (91 FR 41020; FR Doc. 2026-13615) seeking comment on its Draft Guidance for Reducing Risk from Perfluorooctanoic Acid (PFOA) and Perfluorooctane Sulfonic Acid (PFOS) in Biosolids (Docket EPA-HQ-OW-2026-2509). The memorandum provides non-binding, voluntary recommendations for wastewater-treatment facility operators, landowners and farmers, State and Tribal water agencies, and the public — including avoiding land application near certain waters or where young children may have access, and favoring lower-exposure agricultural uses over higher-exposure crops or practices. No numeric federal PFOA/PFOS limit for biosolids is established.

Comment deadline: EPA's dedicated PFAS-in-sewage-sludge page now states that comments are due October 5, 2026, extended from the original September 4 deadline. Some broader EPA pages still display the earlier date, but the PFAS-specific program page expressly identifies Oct. 5 as the extension. Source: EPA PFAS in Sewage Sludge. PFAS fish-consumption advisories are generally issued by state, Tribal, or territorial programs, with PFOS currently serving as a principal PFAS basis for advisories in a number of jurisdictions. Federal EPA criteria, monitoring guidance, analytical methods, and toxicity information provide scientific inputs but do not establish a single national PFAS fish-consumption advisory standard.

Federal framework:

  • Aquatic life: in October 2024, EPA published final national recommended freshwater acute and chronic aquatic-life criteria for PFOA and PFOS, including chronic tissue-based criteria (fish muscle, fish whole-body, invertebrate tissue), plus acute saltwater benchmarks for PFOA/PFOS and acute freshwater benchmarks for eight additional data-limited PFAS. These §304(a) recommendations are not themselves binding state water-quality standards. Source: Federal Register, Oct 7, 2024.
  • Human health: EPA's draft human-health water-quality criteria for PFOA, PFOS, and PFBS were released in December 2024. After two extensions, the comment period closed April 29, 2025; EPA continues to identify the criteria as draft. Source: EPA Human Health Water Quality Criteria for PFAS.
  • Fish and shellfish monitoring: EPA's 2024 advisory-program guidance identifies five PFAS — PFDA, PFHxS, PFNA, PFOA, and PFOS — to monitor for advisories, and seven additional PFAS to monitor to watch.
  • Analytical method: EPA currently recommends Method 1633A for PFAS analysis in fish and shellfish tissue (see CWA Analytical Methods card for current Part 136 status).

Selected state/regional developments:

  • Great Lakes: the Great Lakes Consortium for Fish Consumption Advisories updated its PFOS Best Practice guidance in 2025 using newer toxicity and fish-consumption information, including EPA's 2024 PFOS toxicity assessment. The guidance provides PFOS fish-tissue concentrations corresponding to meal-frequency categories but is nonbinding; individual member jurisdictions determine their own advisories.
  • Virginia: Virginia has issued PFOS-based fish-consumption advisories for several waters (see VA card). Its draft 2026 Integrated Report, released June 15, 2026, includes proposed PFAS-related impaired-water listings under CWA §303(d); related litigation concerning EPA's treatment of PFOS data in Virginia's prior Integrated Report remains pending (see Wild Virginia v. EPA).

PFAS fish-advisory thresholds, monitored compounds, water-quality criteria, and assessment approaches vary substantially by jurisdiction and change as new fish-tissue data and toxicity information become available — see the individual state cards for current state-specific requirements and advisories.

Practice relevance: PFAS fish-tissue data may be relevant to aquatic exposure-pathway evaluation and human-health or ecological risk assessment. Depending on the jurisdiction and waterbody, fish-tissue findings may also inform fish-consumption advisories, surface-water assessments, or Clean Water Act impaired-waters listings.

FDA has not yet promulgated a PFAS-specific bottled-water standard of quality. The firms were added February 18, 2025 and were the first listed under the alert. FDA is reviewing additional bivalve-mollusk sampling data to determine whether further risk-management actions are warranted.

Infant formula: on April 29, 2026, FDA released results from 312 infant-formula samples covering 16 brands, each tested for 30 PFAS as part of a broader contaminant survey (concentrations reported in formula as prepared for feeding). Twenty-five of the 30 PFAS were not detected in any sample; five were detected in subsets at concentrations of 0.51–150 ppt depending on compound. PFOS was the most frequently detected, appearing at detectable levels in about half the samples (0.51–6.0 ppt), with 95% of all samples below 2.9 ppt. FDA states the results affirm the overall safety of the U.S. infant-formula supply, while noting some results initiated additional follow-up, including further testing and manufacturer engagement. FDA intends to develop action levels for contaminants in infant formula but has not stated that future action levels will necessarily be PFAS-specific. Source: FDA, Infant Formula Product Testing Results.

The denial is now under APA challenge (see Tucson v. FDA litigation).

Background monitoring: since 2019, FDA has tested over 1,900 food samples for PFAS through its Total Diet Study and targeted surveys; no PFAS has been detected above the method detection limit in over 95% of fresh and processed foods tested, with detections concentrated in seafood.

PFAS Vapor Pathways & Vapor Intrusion
Emerging science · watch item
EPA general VI framework · ITRC technical guidance · state-level activity
Research indicates that some volatile and semivolatile PFAS — including fluorotelomer alcohols (FTOHs) and perfluorooctanesulfonamides — can occur in vapor-phase environmental media. Vapor-forming PFAS have been measured in soil/subslab vapor, landfill gas, and indoor/outdoor air, though different studies measured different compounds in different matrices; methods are being developed and field-tested for additional matrices including sewer gas. TFA has been reported in soil vapor, while evidence for other ultrashort-chain PFAS such as PFPrA in vapor transport is less developed. A complete subsurface-to-indoor-air vapor-intrusion pathway has not yet been conclusively demonstrated in laboratory or field studies. EPA's 2015 general Vapor Intrusion Technical Guide remains the principal federal VI assessment framework (nonbinding); no federal PFAS-specific VI screening levels exist, with limited inhalation-toxicity data a major constraint on developing risk-based values.
Recent developments:
  • ITRC — Jan 2026 fact sheet, "Vapor-forming PFAS and the Vapor Intrusion Pathway" (PDF) — technical fact sheet summarizing the developing science, knowledge gaps in volatility/air-water partitioning data, inhalation toxicity, analytical methods, and subsurface transport; describes PFAS VI as a possible pathway not yet conclusively demonstrated.
  • Hawaii DOH — only known state agency with interim PFAS VI-specific screening levels: shallow-soil-vapor action levels for TFA and PFPrA, and indoor-air action levels for six PFAS (including TFA, PFPrA, PFBA, PFPeA, PFHxA, and 6:2 FTOH). Hawaii does not currently require PFAS VI assessments but says case-by-case assessment may be requested as the science develops.
  • Michigan EGLE — Air Quality Workgroup (page) conducts PFAS air-monitoring, emissions, and permitting work and develops health-based PFAS air screening levels (PFOA/PFOS values updated April 2024, plus additional PFAS criteria) used in air permitting. These are air-permitting criteria, not a PFAS-specific VI screening framework — no VI-specific soil-gas/indoor-air screening levels were identified for Michigan.
  • Analytical methods — Hayes et al. (2025, Environ. Sci.: Atmos. 5:94–109, DOI 10.1039/D4EA00084F, open access) developed and validated a TD-GC/MS/MS method for selected vapor-phase fluorotelomer alcohols and perfluorooctanesulfonamides in soil gas, sewer gas, and indoor air. No standardized routine PFAS vapor method currently exists — the study itself states this explicitly and identifies possible analytical artifacts affecting perfluorocarboxylic acids specifically.

Practice relevance: vapor-forming PFAS may warrant consideration when developing conceptual site models at PFAS-impacted sites, but decisions to sample soil gas or indoor air remain site- and jurisdiction-specific given current data, analytical, and toxicity limitations.

Connecticut regulates PFAS through phased product notification, labeling, and category-specific bans; a firefighting-foam use ban and take-back program; health-based state drinking-water Action Levels for 10 compounds alongside the federal PFAS MCLs; site-specific remediation criteria under the Release-Based Cleanup Program; and PFOS-based fish-consumption advisories at multiple waterbodies. Connecticut has not promulgated its own PFAS drinking-water MCLs. Connecticut also regulates PFAS through biosolids/soil-amendment limits and wastewater permitting not detailed here.
📦 ProductsEnforceable
Public Act 24-59 establishes phased notification, labeling, and sales restrictions for specified products containing intentionally added PFAS. July 1, 2026: manufacturers of covered categories — including apparel, carpets/rugs, cleaning products, cookware, cosmetics, dental floss, fabric treatments, juvenile products, menstruation products, textile furnishings, ski wax, and upholstered furniture — must notify DEEP before sale, and covered PFAS-containing products must be labeled. DEEP's December 1, 2025 order approved phrases including "Contains PFAS," "Made with PFAS," and "Made with intentionally added PFAS." Jan 1, 2028: manufacture, sale, offer for sale, or distribution of the specified categories containing intentionally added PFAS is prohibited, subject to statutory exemptions — not a general PFAS sales ban. Separate disclosure requirements for severe-wet-condition outdoor apparel and turnout gear took effect January 1, 2026. PA 24-59 separately prohibited the sale/use of certain soil amendments made from biosolids or wastewater sludge containing PFAS beginning October 1, 2024.

🔥 Firefighting FoamEnforceable since 2021
Public Act 21-191 banned use of Class B firefighting foam containing intentionally added PFAS for training and testing immediately upon enactment (July 2021), and banned most other uses effective October 1, 2021. Airport-related facilities with fire-suppression systems using PFAS-containing foam were required to implement mitigation measures and, absent a DEEP-approved extension, transition away from fluorinated foam by October 1, 2023 (federally required uses remain allowed until federal law changes). DEEP identified a fluorine-free replacement foam and, together with the Commission on Fire Prevention and Control, operates a mandatory municipal AFFF take-back program that has collected tens of thousands of gallons of PFAS-containing foam concentrate from Connecticut fire departments.

💧 Drinking WaterState Action Levels + federal MCLs
Connecticut has not promulgated independent state PFAS MCLs, but DPH maintains health-based Action Levels for 10 PFAS — advisory guidelines, not regulatory MCLs, established in two rounds (4 in June 2022, 6 more in June 2023): 6:2 Cl-PFESA 2 ppt; 8:2 Cl-PFESA 5 ppt; PFOS 10 ppt; PFNA 12 ppt; PFOA 16 ppt; GenX (HFPO-DA) 19 ppt; PFHxS 49 ppt; PFHxA 240 ppt; PFBS 760 ppt; PFBA 1,800 ppt. Public water systems are also subject to the 2024 federal PFAS NPDWR, which remains in effect while EPA's 2026 proposed revisions are pending. DPH directs community and NTNC systems to complete initial monitoring by April 26, 2027; under the currently effective federal rule, MCL compliance begins April 26, 2029.

🚶 Site RemediationAPS criteria; site-specific approval required
Connecticut's Release-Based Cleanup Regulations (RBCRs), effective March 1, 2026, replaced the former Transfer Act framework — a fundamental shift from transaction-triggered to discovery-triggered cleanup obligations. PFAS are not among the pollutants with promulgated numeric cleanup criteria in the RBCRs, so PFAS remediation continues via the Additional Polluting Substances (APS) process. DEEP currently publishes fast-track criteria applying to the sum of PFOA+PFOS+PFNA+PFHxS+PFHpA: residential direct exposure 1.35 mg/kg; industrial/commercial direct exposure 41 mg/kg; GA pollutant mobility 1.4 µg/kg; GB pollutant mobility 14 µg/kg; groundwater protection 70 ng/L; surface water protection criteria remain in development. Important qualification: these are not automatically applicable statewide standards — fast-track APS criteria may be used only after written DEEP approval for the particular site or release area. DEEP continues to state these legacy summed-PFAS criteria are being updated to reflect DPH's newer individual Action Levels above. Analytical update: Connecticut's Reasonable Confidence Protocol for EPA Method 1633 became effective July 1, 2026, covering PFAS analysis in aqueous, solid, and tissue matrices — superseding older DEEP webpage references to "Draft Method 1633."

🐟 Fish ConsumptionPFOS-specific advisories
Connecticut DPH maintains waterbody- and species-specific fish-consumption advisories based on PFOS. In June 2023, after evaluating PFOS concentrations in fish tissue sampled during 2017–2022 from 14 waterbodies, DPH issued new or modified consumption advice for certain species in 11 waterbodies. Recommendations vary by waterbody and species and include one-meal-per-month and Do Not Eat advice. The program remains dynamic: in February 2025, DPH loosened the Hockanum River advisory in several sections after newer PFOS data showed lower concentrations, while retaining more restrictive advice in specified reaches. DPH's current annual fish-advisory materials should control for the latest waterbody-specific advice; separate statewide mercury guidance also applies.

Florida
Active
Florida has not established separate state PFAS drinking-water MCLs and is currently implementing the federal NPDWR. State PFAS activity now includes an enforceable AFFF phase-out under the Joe Casello Act, mandatory but currently informational-only wastewater/biosolids monitoring, and older provisional PFOA/PFOS cleanup levels that DEP continues to use while Florida's statutory process for formal statewide PFAS cleanup standards remains pending.
🔥 AFFFEnforceable phase-out
HB 1019, the Joe Casello Act (creating s. 376.911, F.S.), signed June 17, 2026, took effect July 1, 2026. PFAS-containing AFFF may no longer be used for nonemergency instruction, training, or testing, and current inventories must be reported to DEP. July 1, 2027: sale, purchase, and distribution are prohibited, and remaining holders must submit disposal plans. July 1, 2029: possession and use are prohibited, subject to exemptions for airports, certain military applications, emergencies where alternatives are unavailable, and specified retrofit projects. Violations can carry civil penalties up to $10,000 per violation per day. DEP is currently developing implementing rules.
Florida DEP PFAS Efforts
⚖️ Wastewater & BiosolidsMandatory monitoring, informational only
Effective July 1, 2026, public entities disposing of domestic wastewater biosolids and treated effluent with a designed average daily flow of 25,000 GPD or more must conduct at least quarterly PFAS sampling and report results to DEP. Results are currently informational only: the statute expressly states that until EPA establishes PFAS water-quality standards and DEP adopts them, the sampling and results may not serve as the basis for DEP enforcement or another cause of action.

💧 Drinking WaterFederal MCL, no state MCL
Florida has not promulgated independent state PFAS MCLs. Community and nontransient noncommunity systems are subject to the 2024 federal NPDWR (4 ppt PFOA/PFOS; 10 ppt PFHxS/PFNA/HFPO-DA; Hazard Index for mixtures). DEP operates under a primacy-extension agreement and assists with monitoring/data, with EPA currently responsible for formal enforcement. EPA's May 2026 proposals to rescind PFHxS/PFNA/HFPO-DA/Hazard Index requirements and extend the PFOA/PFOS compliance deadline to 2031 remain proposed, not final — the 2024 requirements remain in effect for now.

🚶 Soil & GroundwaterProvisional; statewide standards pending
DEP continues to publish and use provisional PFOA/PFOS cleanup target levels originally developed in 2018–2019 and summarized in its March 2022 PFAS Dynamic Plan: 70 ng/L groundwater for PFOA, PFOS, and PFOA+PFOS; soil CTLs of 1.3 mg/kg residential and 25 mg/kg commercial/industrial, with separate leachability values. Statutory overlay (s. 376.91, F.S., enacted 2022): Florida law requires DEP to adopt statewide PFAS cleanup target levels by rule — prioritizing PFOA/PFOS — if EPA has not finalized its own drinking water/groundwater/soil standards by a statutory trigger date; those DEP rules cannot take effect until ratified by the Legislature. That trigger date has been repeatedly extended by subsequent legislation (most recently tied to 2026-session amendments) rather than left at its original 2025 date. Until DEP's rule for a given PFAS constituent is legislatively ratified, the statute also shields governmental entities and water suppliers from certain enforcement actions based solely on that constituent. Net effect: the older provisional CTLs are DEP's current cleanup benchmarks in practice, but they are not the final, legislatively-ratified statewide standard that s. 376.91 contemplates — that process remains pending.

Illinois
Active
Illinois has enforceable PFAS standards across drinking water, groundwater, selected products, and firefighting foam, and now maintains a statewide PFAS fish-tissue monitoring and advisory program. A June 2026 federal-backstop law prevents weaker federal changes from automatically rolling back existing Illinois standards. Part 620 groundwater values also feed directly into Illinois cleanup-program decisions, while additional wastewater-focused PFAS legislation remains pending.

Jan 1, 2032: sale, offer for sale, or distribution of cosmetics, dental floss, juvenile products, menstrual products, and intimate apparel (defined to include underwear, sleepwear, bras, socks, thermals, loungewear, etc.) containing intentionally added PFAS is prohibited, subject to statutory exemptions. Illinois EPA must also report to the General Assembly by Aug 1, 2027 on the feasibility of a future fluoropolymer-review program. 🔥 Firefighting FoamEnforceable since 2022/2025

Any release or proposed testing release of PFAS-containing foam must be reported to the state within 48 hours. Manufacturers must warn fire departments before selling PFAS-containing Class B foam. 💧 Drinking WaterEnforceable; federal anti-rollback protection
Final order Jan 22, 2026: Illinois adopted PFAS MCLs of PFOA 4 ppt, PFOS 4 ppt, PFHxS 10 ppt, PFNA 10 ppt, HFPO-DA/GenX 10 ppt, plus a Hazard Index of 1 for specified mixtures. Initial monitoring must be completed by April 25, 2027; ongoing compliance monitoring begins April 26, 2027; compliance with the MCLs begins April 26, 2029. State-floor protection strengthened June 26, 2026: Public Act 104-0510 (HB 5070, "EPA-Federal Backstop") amended Illinois's identical-in-substance rulemaking law so the Pollution Control Board may not automatically adopt federal changes that are less stringent than existing Illinois standards — a federal revision that eliminates a numeric concentration limit expressly qualifies as a potentially less-stringent change. Illinois EPA separately stated on May 21, 2026 (before this law was signed) that its drinking-water standards would remain in place regardless of federal action. Accordingly, EPA's proposed rescission of the PFHxS, PFNA, GenX, and Hazard Index MCLs would not automatically remove Illinois's existing standards; weakening them would require a separate Illinois rulemaking path.

🚶 Groundwater & CleanupEnforceable
Part 620 groundwater quality standards, effective March 28, 2025: Illinois adopted enforceable Class I standards for six PFAS — PFOA 4 ppt, PFOS 4 ppt, PFHxS 10 ppt, PFNA 10 ppt, HFPO-DA/GenX 10 ppt, and PFBS 2,000 ppt (notably, the federal 2024 rule did not set an individual PFBS MCL, so this is an Illinois-specific value). Indiana
Active
Indiana has not promulgated independent state PFAS drinking-water MCLs or broad consumer-product bans. Its state-specific PFAS framework instead centers on IDEM's risk-based remediation Published Levels, an existing restriction on PFAS-containing firefighting foam used for training, PFOS-based fish-consumption guidance, and a significant 2026 law that constrains how IDEM may use federal risk values and regulate PFAS. 🚶 CleanupNonrule policy / risk-based closure
IDEM's Risk-Based Closure Guide (R2) and Published Level Tables provide risk-based concentrations used across Indiana's remediation programs, including the Voluntary Remediation Program, State Cleanup, Brownfields, and RCRA corrective action. The R2 is formally an agency Nonrule Policy Document — it does not itself have the force of law, but implements Indiana's statutory risk-based cleanup framework and plays a direct role in site investigation, remedy selection, and closure decisions. IDEM currently lists Published Levels for 11 PFAS compounds. The 2025 tables, effective March 28, 2025, incorporated the 2024 federal PFAS MCLs (PFOA, PFOS, PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA/GenX, PFBS) into the relevant groundwater Published Levels and updated additional PFAS values.

Iowa
Active
Iowa has not enacted a broad PFAS consumer-products law or separate state PFAS drinking-water MCLs. Its most distinctive state-specific PFAS framework is the Land Recycling Program, where DNR's rule-based statewide groundwater and soil standards currently include eight PFAS. Public water systems are subject to the federal NPDWR, while DNR continues evaluating whether additional PFAS surface-water criteria are appropriate. No PFAS-specific fish consumption advisory is currently in effect.
🚶 Cleanup / Land RecyclingRule-based, risk-derived
Iowa's Land Recycling Program (LRP) statewide standards are established by rule under 567 IAC Chapter 137, not merely informal screening guidance — compliance with applicable risk criteria is part of obtaining a No Further Action certificate and the statutory liability protections that follow from it under Iowa Code Ch. 455H. The DNR's online Cumulative Risk Calculator publishes these statewide numeric standards for groundwater (protected and non-protected sources, mg/L) and soil (mg/kg) covering 8 PFAS compounds: PFBS, PFBA, PFDA, PFHxS, PFNA, PFOS, PFOA, and PFHxA. Some calculated values are extraordinarily low — for example, PFDA at 0.014 ppt (0.000000014 mg/L) in protected groundwater — so practical application should be confirmed with DNR and considered alongside current analytical reporting capabilities. Certain protected-groundwater values incorporate the federal MCLs directly (4 ppt for PFOA/PFOS, 10 ppt for PFHxS/PFNA).

Maine
Active
Maine regulates PFAS through phased product sales restrictions, firefighting-foam discharge controls and a legislatively required take-back program, state drinking-water standards, a prohibition on land application of wastewater-treatment sludge and certain sludge-derived materials, new PFAS sludge characterization requirements for processing/disposal, remediation guidance and agricultural screening values, and PFOS-based freshwater fish-consumption advisories. The state also continues a statewide investigation of historical sludge and septage land-application sites.
📦 ProductsEnforceable
Title 38 §1614 establishes phased restrictions on products containing intentionally added PFAS. Existing prohibitions cover carpets, rugs, and fabric treatments; additional categories became prohibited January 1, 2026, including cleaning products, cookware, cosmetics, dental floss, juvenile products, menstruation products, most textile articles, ski wax, and upholstered furniture. Artificial turf and specified severe-wet-condition outdoor apparel provisions take effect January 1, 2029. January 1, 2032: the prohibition applies broadly to remaining products containing intentionally added PFAS unless a statutory exemption or approved current unavoidable use (CUU) determination applies. Certain HVAC/refrigeration equipment, refrigerants, foams, and aerosol propellants are addressed beginning January 1, 2040. Chapter 90 establishes the CUU process; DEP received 11 CUU proposals by June 1, 2025, recommended two in July 2025, and the Board adopted those two — both cleaning-product container components, running through Jan 1, 2031 — in October 2025. DEP has said it intends to initiate another CUU rulemaking round for proposals received before May 1, 2026. A separate rule prohibits intentionally added PFAS in specified short-term direct-food-contact packaging made substantially from paper, paperboard, or plant fiber, beginning May 25, 2026.

💧 Drinking WaterEnforceable state MCLs
Maine's interim standard of 20 ppt for the sum of six PFAS (PFOA, PFOS, PFHxS, PFNA, PFHpA, PFDA) remains applicable through April 26, 2027. Effective December 1, 2025, Maine amended its drinking-water rule to incorporate the 2024 federal PFAS framework: PFOA 4 ppt, PFOS 4 ppt, PFHxS 10 ppt, PFNA 10 ppt, and HFPO-DA/GenX 10 ppt (GenX carries its own individual MCL in addition to participating in the mixture calculation below), plus a Hazard Index of 1 for specified mixtures involving PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA, and PFBS (PFBS has no individual MCL; it participates only in the mixture calculation). Federal-change provision: Maine statute (22 M.R.S. §2650-A) writes these specific numeric levels directly into state law rather than cross-referencing whatever the federal rule currently says — accordingly, a subsequent federal rescission or weakening of the equivalent EPA standards would not by itself automatically eliminate Maine's corresponding state requirements.

⚖️ Biosolids / SludgeEnforceable
Maine law prohibits land application of wastewater-treatment sludge and the land application, sale, or distribution of specified compost and agricultural materials containing sludge or septage, subject to statutory exceptions — the prohibition is not conditioned on first proving a particular batch exceeds a PFAS concentration. Enacted 2022 (L.D. 1911, codified 38 MRS §1306(7)) as part of Maine's response to PFAS contamination associated with historical land application. A separate 2021 law requires investigation of historical licensed sludge and septage land-application sites; DEP has identified more than 1,000 such sites and continues phased soil and groundwater investigations — the entire inventory has not been uniformly sampled. Maine has also documented sludge-disposal and management-capacity constraints as land application has ceased and alternative disposal or processing capacity is developed. Maine CDC maintains agricultural PFOS action levels including 210 ppt for milk and 3.4 ppb for beef.

PFAS sludge characterization: On Aug. 27, 2026, DEP notified wastewater-treatment-plant sludge generators and Maine solid-waste facilities that receive sludge of a new requirement under Public Law 2025, ch. 651. A solid-waste facility may not process, beneficially reuse, or dispose of sludge unless the generator has characterized it under a DEP-approved plan that includes PFAS sampling and analysis. Sampling and analysis must begin no later than January 2027. This is a characterization requirement for sludge management and disposal; it does not establish a new statewide numeric PFAS concentration limit for sludge.

Maryland
Active
Maryland regulates PFAS through enacted product and firefighting-foam restrictions, industrial wastewater and pretreatment requirements, phased sewage-sludge land-application controls, and PFOS-based fish-consumption advisories. Public water systems are currently governed by the federal PFAS NPDWR; MDE is developing corresponding state standards designed to align with the federal rule.
📦 ProductsEnforceable
Maryland's George "Walter" Taylor Act (2022, HB 275/SB 273, Chs. 138/139 — named for a firefighter who died after repeated PFAS exposure) restricts PFAS in carpets/rugs and direct-food-contact packaging, among other categories. Since January 1, 2024, intentionally added PFAS are prohibited in carpets/rugs and covered food packaging. Separate laws prohibit specified intentionally added PFAS compounds and salts in cosmetics beginning January 1, 2025 (Ch. 490 of 2021), and prohibit PFAS-containing components in covered playground-surfacing materials beginning October 1, 2024 (HB 1147, a distinct 2024 law, not the food-packaging provision). 2026 legislative proposal: HB 1022/SB 686 proposed broader category-specific PFAS product phaseouts and a manufacturer registration system. HB 1022 was withdrawn April 8, 2026; SB 686 did not advance beyond committee hearing. Neither was enacted.

⚖️ Sewage Sludge / BiosolidsEnforceable, phased
HB 925/SB 719 (Chs. 330/329), signed April 28, 2026, effective October 1, 2026: beginning October 1, 2028, sewage sludge or products containing sewage sludge may not be land applied to agricultural or marginal land when the total concentration of PFOA + PFOS is ≥50 ppb. Material at 25 to <50 ppb is subject to additional management requirements; the law permits (but doesn't mandate) commingling to reduce concentrations below 25 ppb under specified conditions, and separately provides a limited temporary land-application pathway while required source-tracking and mitigation measures are developed. Generators at or above 25 ppb must conduct source tracking and develop an MDE-reviewed mitigation plan intended to reduce concentrations below 25 ppb, with an implementation timeline not to exceed five years from the triggering determination (not simply five years from the Act's effective date). Sampling must occur at least quarterly using EPA Method 1633A or an approved equivalent. The law also authorizes MDE and pretreatment authorities to establish PFAS controls for industrial users where applicable.

📦 ProductsEnforceable beginning 2028

New Mexico

New Mexico regulates PFAS through phased product restrictions, manufacturer reporting and product labeling under the PFAS Protection Act; targeted hazardous-waste controls for discarded PFAS-containing AFFF; federal drinking-water requirements; and NMED risk-assessment guidance used in contaminated-site work. 📦 ProductsEnforceable; phased bans and reporting
New Mexico's PFAS Protection Act implementing rule, 20.13.2 NMAC, became effective July 1, 2026. Beginning January 1, 2027, intentionally added PFAS are prohibited in cookware, food packaging, dental floss, juvenile products, and firefighting foam; nine additional categories (carpets/rugs, cleaning products, cosmetics, upholstered furniture, textiles, and others) follow January 1, 2028. Beginning January 1, 2032, the prohibition applies broadly to remaining products unless exempt or covered by an approved currently unavoidable use (CUU) determination. Covered products manufactured for sale after January 1, 2027 must generally carry PFAS labeling (an Erlenmeyer-flask symbol with "PFAS" text) — a requirement broader than the sales ban itself, since it also applies to many otherwise-exempt products — subject to specified exemptions (certain medical, veterinary, and federally preempted products). Deadlines: CUU requests affecting the 2027 prohibitions are due October 31, 2026, with final NMED determinations due March 1, 2027 (complete requests deemed approved pending review); label-waiver requests submitted by October 31, 2026 receive a separate determination deadline of June 1, 2027. Initial manufacturer reports are due January 1, 2027 (not at the July 2026 effective date). Penalties: up to $15,000 civil penalty, escalating up to $25,000/day for continued non-compliance with a compliance order. The rule permits shared-state reporting arrangements and waivers based on substantially equivalent publicly available information (e.g., Minnesota's PRISM system) — so a Minnesota filing doesn't automatically satisfy New Mexico, but NMED has discretion to accept it. Exemption review: House Joint Memorial 3 (2026) requests NMED evaluate the health, environmental, and economic implications of statutory PFAS exemptions (including fluoropolymers) and recommend whether they should be retained, modified, or removed — preliminary findings requested by Dec 1, 2026, final report due Aug 1, 2027.

Relevance: an example of a water utility (rather than a state AG or private plaintiff) seeking cost recovery directly from upstream industrial dischargers for its own PFAS-treatment capital and O&M costs — a distinct plaintiff category from the AG-enforcement and personal-injury suits elsewhere in this section, and one to watch for whether other utilities with recently built treatment infrastructure bring similar claims. Town of Pine Hill, Alabama v. 3M Co. — 11th Circuit Vacates Remand Order (July 29, 2026)
Jul 29, 2026Appellate ruling · Federal officer removal statute · No. 25-10746 (11th Cir.)
Pine Hill, Alabama sued 3M and other parties in state court over Alabama River contamination, alleging PFAS releases tied to PFAS supplied to paper mills; the town's complaint expressly disclaimed claims connected to aqueous film-forming foam (AFFF) made for the U.S. military. 3M removed the case to federal court under the federal officer removal statute, arguing that PFAS from AFFF it manufactured for the government may also be a source of the contamination at issue. The district court remanded to state court, finding the gravamen of the complaint concerned PFAS supplied to paper mills rather than federal conduct. On July 29, 2026, an Eleventh Circuit panel (Circuit Judge Charles R. Wilson, joined by Judges Newsom and Kidd) vacated the remand order, holding that the district court should have credited 3M's theory that the alleged contamination relates to PFAS contamination broadly rather than only to the paper-mill supply chain, and that Pine Hill's AFFF disclaimer functioned as artful pleading rather than a genuine narrowing of the claims. The panel found 3M plausibly alleged facts supporting all three elements of federal officer removal jurisdiction, including that PFAS-containing AFFF was supplied to Maxwell Air Force Base and that groundwater at the base contained PFAS consistent with AFFF releases flowing toward the Alabama River. The case returns to the district court for further proceedings on the jurisdictional record. Relevance: the ruling addresses how courts define the scope of a complaint's “gravamen” when a defendant's federal-contractor PFAS activity (AFFF) and non-federal PFAS activity (industrial supply) occur in the same watershed — a fact pattern relevant to source-apportionment and exposure-pathway analysis at sites with multiple PFAS-use histories. Sources: 11th Circuit opinion (Justia) · 🔒 InsideEPA coverage.

EPA/DOJ/WV Settlement with Chemours — $450M Federal PFAS Enforcement (June 24, 2026)
📌 PinnedJun 24, 2026Federal enforcement settlement · First comprehensive federal PFAS settlement with a manufacturer
On June 24, 2026, the U.S. Department of Justice, U.S. EPA, and the West Virginia Department of Environmental Protection announced a proposed consent decree with The Chemours Company resolving allegations of PFAS releases at three facilities: Washington Works (West Virginia), Fayetteville Works (North Carolina), and Chambers Works (New Jersey). The proposed settlement totals more than $450 million and asserts claims under the Clean Water Act, Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, Toxic Substances Control Act, and the West Virginia Water Pollution Control Act. The settlement permits Chemours to continue manufacturing PFAS for “critical commercial and military applications.” The consent decree is subject to final court approval; the Federal Register notice (91 FR — FR Doc 2026-13081) opening the 30-day public comment period was published June 29, 2026, with written comments due July 29, 2026. FC, LLC, 178 F.4th 102 (4th Cir. 2026), the Fourth Circuit vacated a district court preliminary injunction that had restrained Chemours from discharging HFPO-DA above its NPDES permit limits at the same Washington Works facility. About this tracker & methodology

This tracker is a curated, public-facing reference covering federal, state, international, industry, and selected legal PFAS developments. It is produced by Library Services at Integral Consulting Inc. and is free to share. The tracker is designed as a discovery and orientation tool: substantive entries point readers to the underlying agency, legislative, court, or other source whenever available.

This page explains the curation gate, editorial workflow, source practices, and AI-assisted review used to maintain the tracker. The methodology is intentionally written at a durable level so that the scope can evolve without changing the underlying editorial standard.

Inclusion criteria (curation gate)

Candidate developments are screened for material regulatory, implementation, monitoring, enforcement, legal, or industry significance. Examples that generally clear the gate include:

  • Finalized rules, newly effective requirements, or material changes to implementation deadlines or obligations
  • Legislation that is enacted or has substantially advanced and is likely to affect PFAS compliance or oversight
  • Major agency guidance, monitoring/data releases, enforcement actions, or program implementation changes
  • Substantive court rulings, settlements, or procedural decisions with material regulatory or technical significance
  • Durable agency, scientific, or legal syntheses that improve understanding of the PFAS regulatory landscape
  • Material status corrections identified during source verification or card review

Items generally do not clear the gate merely because they are newly reported. Routine filings, advocacy letters, conference discussions, stakeholder comments, speculative coverage, minor source corrections, and other signals are normally kept in the underlying card, Key Dates, Trending Now, the News feed, or the 👀 On the radar watch rather than promoted to Recent Updates.

How the tracker sections work

  • Recent Updates. Records material changes added in the current tracker release. The displayed date is the tracker posting date, not necessarily the date of the underlying event. Prior-week entries do not roll forward simply because the underlying issue remains important.
  • Federal, State, International, and Selected Legal cards. Hold the substantive status, context, and source links. A card may receive a material update even when the underlying event occurred earlier; if that change matters to readers, it is also surfaced in Recent Updates.
  • Trending Now. Highlights themes showing sustained public momentum across multiple developments. Trending is observational and does not imply endorsement, prediction, or a view on the merits.
  • 👀 On the radar. Identifies developing issues being watched for a clearer regulatory, legal, or implementation signal before they are promoted to Recent Updates.
  • News. Provides selected trade-press context and points back to the substantive tracker card. News coverage is not treated as the authoritative source for a rule, ruling, or agency action where a primary source is available.
  • Reference Shelf. Preserves durable agency guidance, datasets, analyses, and other resources likely to remain useful beyond the current news cycle.

Editorial workflow (human-in-the-loop)

  1. Recurring scans. Federal, state, international, Federal Register, trade-press, and selected legal sources are reviewed on a recurring basis for candidate developments.
  2. Full state-card review. Each state card is reviewed during the weekly cycle even when no new state development is found. The review checks for stale status language, passed deadlines, changed bill or rule status, newly effective requirements, pending decisions that have been resolved, and outdated source links.
  3. Primary-source verification. Material developments are checked against agency publications, Federal Register notices, legislative text/status records, court documents, or other authoritative sources whenever available. Trade press and secondary sources are used for discovery and context.
  4. AI-assisted candidate identification. AI tools help sift public-source volume, compare current information with existing tracker text, and draft candidate summaries for human review.
  5. Human editorial review. Library Services applies the curation gate, verifies the source, decides whether a change belongs in the tracker, and edits the wording for accuracy and neutrality.
  6. Editorial-neutrality check. Language is reviewed for unsupported significance claims, advocacy framing, predictions, and characterizations of parties' conduct. Allegations and stakeholder positions are attributed.
  7. Pre-publication quality control. The rendered tracker is reviewed for stale dates, internal links, source links, duplicate identifiers, section placement, filters, and other structural issues before publication.

AI use disclosure

The tracker is AI-assisted. AI may be used to:

  • Sift public sources for candidate developments
  • Compare new source material with existing tracker language to identify possible stale content
  • Draft candidate summaries for human review
  • Identify cross-cutting themes for Trending Now and watch items
  • Support structural and link-quality checks before publication

AI is not used to:

  • Render expert opinions or legal advice
  • Characterize the merits or conduct of parties in litigation or regulatory matters
  • Make recommendations to readers
  • Process confidential client information for this public tracker

Every substantive tracker change is human-reviewed before publication. The AI-Assisted, Human-Curated Tracker label reflects that workflow.

Known limitations and bias mitigation

No scan is exhaustive. Public sources may be incomplete, delayed, paywalled, revised, or inconsistent across agency pages. AI tools can also over-weight recency, miss context, or generate plausible but inaccurate text. Trade press, law-firm publications, advocacy materials, and other secondary sources reflect their own editorial perspectives.

Library Services mitigates these limitations through primary-source verification, recurring card review, cross-source checks where needed, neutral rewriting, explicit attribution, and human review of every substantive change. These practices reduce but do not eliminate error or bias.

Source citation policy

Citing items from this tracker:

When using a regulatory fact, court ruling, monitoring result, or other tracker content in professional work, cite the underlying primary source rather than the tracker whenever possible. The tracker is a discovery and navigation aid; the original source remains the authoritative reference.

Value judgments, predictions, unsupported significance claims, and characterizations of parties' conduct are excluded. Defendants, plaintiffs, agencies, regulators, industry groups, and advocacy organizations are treated under the same editorial standard.
