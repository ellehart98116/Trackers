PFAS Destruction & Disposal Guidance
Interim guidance · non-binding
EPA OLEM · Updated 2026 from 2024 baseline
April 28, 2026 update — EPA published FR Doc 2026-08174 opening 60-day public comment on the updated 2026 Interim Guidance (superseding the April 2024 version). The 2026 guidance evaluates and ranks destruction/disposal technologies by release potential for the first time, covering high-temperature incineration, smoldering combustion, electrochemical oxidation, supercritical water oxidation, permitted Class I underground injection wells, permitted RCRA Subtitle C hazardous-waste landfills, and landfill containment. TSCA New-Chemical Review — Data-Center Immersion-Cooling Fluid (Opteon 2P50)
PMN under EPA review · comment period closed Jul 15, 2026
EPA OPPT New Chemicals Program · TSCA §5 Premanufacture Notice · Docket EPA-HQ-OPPT-2025-2932
EPA is reviewing a premanufacture notice (PMN) for 3-Hexene, 1,1,1,2,2,5,5,6,6,6-decafluoro-, (3E) (CASRN 1256353-26-0), a fluorinated immersion-cooling fluid marketed by Chemours as Opteon 2P50 for data-center equipment cooling (the PMN applicant's identity is claimed as confidential business information). The applicant sought priority review under EPA's September 2025 policy fast-tracking new-chemical reviews for AI/data-center projects pursuant to Executive Order 14318 — this is reportedly the first chemical for which industry has invoked that fast-track pathway. This PMN is subject to the full TSCA §5 review EPA now requires for new PFAS following its May 2026 curtailment of the Low-Volume Exemption pathway (see TSCA 8(a)(7) card above).

Definitional dispute: Chemours has stated (2023 product launch) that it does not consider Opteon 2P50 a PFAS, describing it as a hydrofluoroolefin (HFO) that degrades quickly in the atmosphere. A 17-organization coalition led by Earthjustice, in comments filed July 15, 2026 responding to EPA's notice of receipt of the PMN, contends HFOs including this substance break down in the atmosphere into shorter-chain PFAS — principally perfluoropropanoic acid (PFPrA) and TFA — that are persistent and highly mobile in water. The coalition cites ECHA's RAC June 2026 opinion proposing to classify TFA as a reproductive toxicant (now before the European Commission) — the same opinion already noted on this tracker's EU/Germany TFA card.

Other arguments in the coalition's comments: that TSCA §5 requires EPA to assess unreasonable risk "without consideration of costs or other nonrisk factors," making the applicant's data-center-benefit rationale for priority review legally irrelevant to the risk determination itself; that EPA's exposure modeling should not assume worker use of protective equipment and should account for aggregate exposure across many facilities and other marketed uses (the comments assert the fluid is also marketed for electric-vehicle battery cooling); and that the applicant improperly claimed CBI protection over toxicity-profile and occupational-exposure data that TSCA generally does not allow to be withheld — an argument that parallels separate, ongoing Earthjustice-affiliated litigation over EPA's CBI-disclosure practices in PMN reviews generally. Chemours has responded publicly that its cooling systems are closed-loop with low fugitive emissions and that EPA's new-chemicals review is a rigorous process; it has not directly addressed the coalition's PFAS-classification argument in public statements found to date.

Relevance: the PMN review's outcome will affect the immersion-cooling fluid market broadly (3M's 2025 exit from PFAS manufacturing removed the prior generation of cooling fluids, Novec and Fluorinert, creating the supply gap Opteon 2P50 is intended to fill) and could set precedent for how EPA treats HFO/PFAS-adjacent chemistries under the data-center fast-track policy. Advocacy groups (e.g., Food & Water Watch) have separately alleged that Chemours plans to expand PFAS production capacity at its Fayetteville, NC and Parkersburg, WV facilities — the same sites at issue in the Chemours $450M settlement — to meet data-center demand; this tracker has not independently confirmed that claim against a primary source (e.g., a permit filing or SEC disclosure) as of this refresh. Sources: EPA Federal Register notice of PMN receipt · The Guardian · Verdant Law analysis.

Federal Biosolids — PFOA/PFOS Risk Assessment & Draft Voluntary Guidance
Draft · comments open through Sept 4, 2026
EPA OW · CWA Section 405 · Docket EPA-HQ-OW-2026-2509
🚨 Published Jul 6, 2026: Draft voluntary guidance in the Federal Register — 91 FR 41020; comments close September 4, 2026.

EPA published in the Federal Register (July 6, 2026) a request for comment on the Draft Guidance for Reducing Risk from Perfluorooctanoic Acid (PFOA) and Perfluorooctane Sulfonic Acid (PFOS) in Biosolids (Docket EPA-HQ-OW-2026-2509). The draft memorandum provides non-binding, voluntary recommendations for wastewater treatment plant operators, landowners and farmers, and State/Tribal water agencies — e.g., avoiding land application of biosolids near waterways or on lands where children play, and avoiding use on crops with higher human-exposure potential. No numeric federal biosolids threshold is established. 60-day comment period.

The Federal Register notice states that EPA has determined the Biden-era January 14, 2025 Draft Risk Assessment "exhibited a number of flaws" — specifically the absence of a national occurrence survey, the exclusive focus on higher-risk hypothetical scenarios "that do not reflect the majority of land application in the U.S.," and the 1 ppb starting concentration that EPA now emphasizes "was not intended to be interpreted as a 'safe level.'" EPA is soliciting feedback on next steps for the Draft Risk Assessment and on non-risk-based source-reduction approaches implemented by several states. Any future rulemaking would come after these decisions and would proceed under CWA § 405 if warranted.

Land-application context (from FR notice): approximately 60% of the nation's sewage sludge is land-applied as a soil conditioner or fertilizer (~32% agricultural, ~28% other lands including reclamation sites, home lawns, and golf courses); ~25% is disposed of in monofills or MSW landfills; ~14% is incinerated.

UCMR 6 — Proposed Rule (ultrashort organofluorines)
Proposed · comments open
EPA OW · SDWA § 1445(a)(2) · monitoring 2028–2030
EPA released the pre-publication Federal Register notice for the Sixth Unregulated Contaminant Monitoring Rule (UCMR 6) on June 26–30, 2026. UCMR 6 would require public water systems (all CWSs and NTNCWSs serving >10,000, all CWSs/NTNCWSs serving 3,300–10,000, and a representative sample of 800 systems serving <3,300) to monitor for 30 unregulated contaminants during a 12-month period between January 2028 and December 2030.

Seven ultrashort organofluorine compounds are included, all analyzed by EPA Method 563. EPA classifies four as PFAS under the CCL 5 structural definition — PFPrS, PFPrA, PFEtS, and PFMOAA — and three (TFA, TFMS, TFSI) as non-PFAS but includes them for monitoring "due to their persistence in the environment, adverse health effects, mobility, and wide use," and because Method 563 can capture them. Other contaminants in UCMR 6 include chlorinated solvents, chemical intermediates, pesticides, and pesticide metabolites analyzed by EPA Methods 524.3, 525.3, and 540.

Public webinars August 11 & 12, 2026. Public comments due August 31, 2026 (Docket EPA-HQ-OW-2023-0469). Large PWSs (>10,000 people) are responsible for their own analytical costs; EPA covers costs for small PWSs, subject to annual appropriations and laboratory capacity. EPA declined petitions to add microplastics and pharmaceuticals to UCMR 6, citing the absence of a validated drinking-water analytical method — including a petition from the governors of seven states (Connecticut, Delaware, Illinois, Maryland, Michigan, New Jersey, and Wisconsin). EPA noted microplastics have separately been added to the draft CCL 6 (see CCL 6 card) and remain a research focus.

Definitional context: EPA's inclusion of TFA for monitoring sits alongside its June 30–July 1, 2026 pesticide-registration actions, which included three new single-fluorinated-carbon active ingredients (epyrifenacil, diflufenican, trifludimoxazin) treated as non-PFAS under the Office of Pesticide Programs' definition, alongside a separate expanded-use approval for bifenthrin (an existing, unrelated active ingredient). Environmentalists (CBD, EWG) have noted the single-fluorinated-carbon definitional split. In the EU, ECHA's RAC has separately recommended classifying TFA as reproductive toxicant 1B and PMT/vPvM.

⚖️ Litigation watch:

CBD/CFS lawsuit challenges EPA's approval of isocycloseram as a pesticide, reportedly the second such challenge on PFAS-definitional grounds: The Center for Biological Diversity and the Center for Food Safety contend isocycloseram meets a PFAS structural definition and that EPA's approval violates the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act (FIFRA) and the Endangered Species Act (ESA). EPA maintains that isocycloseram, like the pesticide-registration decisions referenced above, does not meet the agency's own narrower PFAS definition used in its pesticide-registration program. The case tests, in a pesticide-registration context, the same definitional split visible in this card's TFA coverage. Plaintiffs' opening brief is due Aug. 21, 2026. Announced · grants finalized
EPA Office of the Administrator · Bipartisan Infrastructure Law § 50105
In late June and early July 2026, EPA issued a series of press releases framing a "comprehensive PFAS strategy" and finalized the fifth (and final) annual installment of the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law Emerging Contaminants in Small or Disadvantaged Communities program, distributing the ~$5 billion cumulative allocation to more than 40 states and territories for testing, planning, and drinking-water infrastructure investments.

Strategy commitments include:

The strategy statements pair the May 2026 NPDWR compliance-extension and rescission proposals (see NPDWR card) with continued investment in treatment infrastructure and discharge controls at the source. PFAS Effluent Limitation Guidelines (ELGs)
Proposed · multiple sectors advancing
EPA OW · Clean Water Act § 304(b) · 40 CFR Parts 414, 433, 463, 466, etc.
EPA is advancing PFAS-specific industrial wastewater discharge limits under the Clean Water Act effluent guidelines program. Active rulemakings:
  • OCPSF (Organic Chemicals, Plastics & Synthetic Fibers, 40 CFR Part 414) — proposed rule published January 23, 2025 introducing PFAS effluent limits for facilities that manufacture PFAS. Public comment period closed; EPA reviewing.
  • Metal Finishing & Electroplating (40 CFR Parts 433 + 413) — PFAS limits under consideration; EPA's Effluent Guidelines Program Plan identified these categories for revision.
  • Landfill Leachate (40 CFR Part 445) — preliminary study underway; landfills are a known PFAS pathway via leachate to POTWs and surface water.
  • Centralized Waste Treatment — under EPA review for PFAS handling at CWT facilities (often the destination for landfill leachate and ion-exchange regeneration).
Once finalized, ELGs flow into NPDES permits for direct dischargers and into pretreatment standards for indirect dischargers through POTWs. for industrial wastewater consulting, permit renewals, and treatment-system design, ELGs are the trigger that moves PFAS from "monitor only" to "comply with a numeric limit." Engineers designing treatment trains (ion exchange, GAC, RO, foam fractionation) need to factor in the discharge-limit endpoint, not just the source-control or treatment-feasibility question. Aquatic exposure pathway · State-led / federally informed

States are increasingly issuing PFAS fish consumption advisories, primarily for PFOS, drawing on a federal scientific framework still being developed. Coverage spans freshwater and coastal marine environments and reflects research on PFAS bioaccumulation in organisms and biomagnification through the food chain.

Federal framework (existing and pending):

  • Aquatic life water quality criteria for PFOA and PFOS, plus acute freshwater benchmarks for eight additional PFAS, finalized by EPA in October 2024.

PFAS Vapor Pathways & Vapor Intrusion
Emerging · watch item
ITRC + state-level activity · No federal PFAS-specific VI guidance yet
Vapor-forming PFAS (notably fluorotelomer alcohols (FTOHs), perfluoroalkyl sulfonamides, and ultrashort-chain PFAS including TFA and PFPrA) have been detected in soil gas, sewer gas, outdoor air, and indoor air — but a complete subsurface-to-indoor-air VI pathway has not yet been conclusively demonstrated. The 2015 OSWER VI Technical Guide remains the operative federal framework; no federal PFAS-specific VI screening levels exist.
Recent developments:
  • ITRC — Jan 2026 Fact Sheet "Vapor-forming PFAS" (PDF) — formal technical synthesis of vapor-forming PFAS and VI pathway considerations, knowledge gaps in vapor pressure / Henry's law data, and inhalation toxicology needs.
  • Hawaii DOH — only state with interim PFAS VI screening levels (two subsurface vapor + six indoor air screening levels, including for TFA and PFPrA). Interim guidance does not yet require PFAS-VI assessments but signals direction.
  • Michigan EGLE — Air Quality Workgroup (page) conducts PFAS air-quality investigations at AFFF / industrial sites; no codified PFAS-VI screening levels yet.
  • Analytical method development — vapor-phase PFAS sampling for soil gas, sewer gas, and indoor air now described in the peer-reviewed literature (Schauer et al., RSC Environ. Sci. Atmos., 2025).
Integral risk assessors and site-characterization teams supporting AFFF sites, chemical manufacturing facilities, and PFAS-impacted redevelopment should be aware that ITRC has formalized the vapor pathway as a credible-but-unproven concern. Expect inquiries from clients about whether to add soil gas or indoor air to PFAS sampling plans; the analytical methods now exist, even where screening levels do not.

RAC final opinion · June 1–5, 2026
ECHA Risk Assessment Committee · CLP Regulation · Dossier from German federal authorities (BfC/UBA/BfR)
At its June 1–5, 2026 meeting, ECHA’s Risk Assessment Committee (RAC) adopted a final scientific opinion classifying trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and its salts as Reproductive Toxicity Category 1B (H360Df) and as a PMT/vPvM substance (Persistent, Mobile, and Toxic / very Persistent and very Mobile), following a dossier submitted by German federal authorities (BfC, UBA, BfR). FDA denies citizen petition for PFAS food tolerance/action levels
Final agency action · Jun 17, 2026
FDA Office of Food Chemical Safety, Dietary Supplements and Innovation · FD&C Act §§ 402(a)(1) and 406
On June 17, 2026 (publicly reported June 24), FDA issued a 27-page response denying a 2023 citizen petition (filed by the Tucson Environmental Justice Task Force and co-petitioners) that sought temporary tolerance levels — or alternatively non-regulatory action levels — for PFAS residues in food. The original petition requested tolerance levels at the “method detection limit” for 30 PFAS in head- and leaf-lettuce and blueberries, and for 26 PFAS in ready-to-eat bread, milk, eggs, salmon, clams, corn silage, and corn snaplage.
  • Petitioners sued FDA in January 2025 over the agency’s failure to act; FDA later agreed to a court-sanctioned response deadline, subsequently extended to June 30, 2026.
  • FDA’s stated basis for denial: insufficient data and analytical methods to meet the criteria for a tolerance listing; lack of toxicological reference values (TRVs) for some PFAS cited; and for PFAS with TRVs, insufficient data to assess achievability at the levels requested.
  • Door left open: FDA stated it is “currently considering setting action levels for PFAS in some products” and that it will reevaluate as more data become available.
  • Method context: In April 2024, FDA formally issued an LC-MS/MS method for measuring 30 PFAS in food and feed, with validation data.
  • Tolerance levels are rare: FDA has only set such levels for polychlorinated biphenyls. Tolerances are set via rulemaking; action levels are set via guidance.
Practice implications: The denial confirms that, as of mid-2026, there are no federal tolerance or action levels for PFAS in food. Food, biosolids land-application, and agricultural clients should continue to rely on existing FDA guidance and state-level actions; product-stewardship work on food-contact PFAS remains governed by state product bans and existing FDA food-contact authorizations. Update: petitioners filed a supplemental complaint July 31, 2026 challenging this denial in court; see Tucson v. FDA card for details.

FDA PFAS in Bottled Water — Rulemaking Triggered by EPA MCLs
Pre-rulemaking · statutory deadline late 2028
FDA · FD&C Act § 410; 21 CFR 165.110 (standard of quality for bottled water)
Under FD&C Act § 410, when EPA establishes a maximum contaminant level for a substance in public drinking water, FDA must set a corresponding bottled-water standard unless it determines the contaminant is not present in bottled water. EPA's April 2024 NPDWR finalizing MCLs for six PFAS (4–10 ppt thresholds) triggered this obligation. FDA's PFAS webpage identifies bottled water as one of three explicit priority areas for future risk management action, alongside seafood and infant formula (see below). FDA states it is reviewing EPA's regulations, including the pending rescission proposal, to determine what allowable levels are appropriate; the agency has a statutory deadline of late 2028 to finalize standards, after which EPA's drinking-water MCLs would automatically apply to bottled water if FDA has not acted. Complicating factor: EPA's pending proposal to rescind MCLs for four of the six PFAS (retaining only PFOA and PFOS) would, if finalized, narrow the set of compounds that triggered FDA's obligation in the first place; FDA has not yet clarified how the proposed rescission affects its bottled-water rulemaking timeline or scope.

FDA's own 2023–2024 bottled-water testing (results shared April 2025): FDA tested 197 domestic and imported bottled water samples (purified, artesian, spring, and mineral waters) collected at retail for 18 PFAS, including the six with EPA MCLs. Ten samples had detectable PFAS (eight domestic, one to four PFAS each; two imported artesian, one to two PFAS each); none exceeded EPA's MCLs. Two of the detected PFAS have no established EPA MCL. Prior FDA surveys (2016; ongoing Total Diet Study sampling) had not detected PFAS in bottled water.

FDA's other two priority areas: Seafood — FDA continues enforcement via Import Alert 99-48 (detention without physical examination), which as of this refresh lists eight firms for PFOA contamination in imported clams; FDA is reviewing a bivalve-mollusk sampling assignment (clams, oysters, mussels, scallops) to evaluate further action. Infant formula — following the April 2026 Operation Stork Speed testing (see Total Diet Study card), FDA plans continued testing and states it will work to establish action levels for PFAS in infant formula specifically.

Relevance: bottled-water manufacturers and importers should track both the FDA rulemaking and the EPA NPDWR rescission in parallel, since the scope of FDA's eventual standard depends on which PFAS retain federal drinking-water MCLs; seafood importers (particularly clam processors/distributors) should confirm Import Alert 99-48 status for their facilities. Sources: FDA PFAS webpage · FDA bottled-water analytical results · McGuireWoods Contaminants Compass, July 2026.

EPA/DOJ/WV Settlement with Chemours — $450M Federal PFAS Enforcement (June 24, 2026)
📌 PinnedJun 24, 2026Federal enforcement settlement · First comprehensive federal PFAS settlement with a manufacturer
On June 24, 2026, the U.S. Department of Justice, U.S. EPA, and the West Virginia Department of Environmental Protection announced a proposed consent decree with The Chemours Company resolving allegations of PFAS releases at three facilities: Washington Works (West Virginia), Fayetteville Works (North Carolina), and Chambers Works (New Jersey). The proposed settlement totals more than $450 million and asserts claims under the Clean Water Act, Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, Toxic Substances Control Act, and the West Virginia Water Pollution Control Act. Settlement components: $22.5 million civil penalty; ~$90 million multi-year PFAS discharge mitigation program; ~$60 million in PFAS pollution controls for surface-water discharges and air emissions at the West Virginia facility; ~$280 million to supply clean drinking water for more than a decade to communities surrounding the West Virginia and New Jersey facilities; and a separate obligation to evaluate options to reduce PFAS releases from the North Carolina facility. The settlement permits Chemours to continue manufacturing PFAS for “critical commercial and military applications.” The consent decree is subject to final court approval; the Federal Register notice (91 FR — FR Doc 2026-13081) opening the 30-day public comment period was published June 29, 2026, with written comments due July 29, 2026. The settlement does not extend to DuPont, which owned the facilities prior to spinning off Chemours in 2015. State pushback (July 2026 update): North Carolina officials and the state attorney general have publicly criticized the settlement's fund allocation, arguing the bulk of relief funds — roughly $280M for alternative drinking water plus a ~$90M discharge-mitigation program — is weighted toward West Virginia and will do little to remediate Cape Fear River contamination. The consent decree does require GenX release controls at ≥99% efficiency and third-party-engineered controls at the North Carolina facility. July 15, 2026 update: NC Attorney General Jeff Jackson issued a public request for North Carolina residents to submit comments to the federal docket during the 30-day public comment window (comments due July 29). The AG's release states that the decree was negotiated without state involvement and does not provide direct benefit to North Carolina. The AG indicated that North Carolina will continue its separate state-court action against Chemours regardless of the federal consent-decree outcome. Related Fourth Circuit ruling on Washington Works (June 3, 2026): In West Virginia Rivers Coalition, Inc. v. Chemours Co. FC, LLC, 178 F.4th 102 (4th Cir. 2026), the Fourth Circuit vacated a district court preliminary injunction that had restrained Chemours from discharging HFPO-DA above its NPDES permit limits at the same Washington Works facility. The Fourth Circuit narrowed application of the Winter irreparable-harm factor in CWA citizen-suit preliminary injunctions, holding that (a) harm to the general public without harm to the movant is insufficient; (b) irreparable harm cannot be presumed from CWA permit violations alone; and (c) expert testimony describing harm as "likelier" (rather than "more likely than not") from tap-water exposure at ≥10 ppt HFPO-DA did not meet the preliminary-injunction standard. Associational standing was affirmed. The proceeding is separate from the $450M federal consent decree covered by this card but concerns the same facility. Outcome not yet confirmed as of this refresh. Sources: DOJ press release · EPA press release · Chemours investor release.
Cavalier et al. v. Apple, Inc. — PFAS Class Action Standing & RSL Precedent (N.D. Cal., March 16, 2026)
📌 PinnedMar 16, 2026Consumer fraud class action · Motion to dismiss denied
In Cavalier et al. v. Apple, Inc. (N.D. Cal.), the court denied Apple's motion to dismiss a PFAS false-advertising class action alleging that fluoroelastomer watch bands contained PFHxA at concentrations exceeding Apple's own supplier-policy thresholds (internal RSL flagged PFHxA "restricted" at 25 ppb; plaintiff testing showed >1,000 ppb). Four holdings with broad implications: (1) Article III standing via representative-product testing (one post-filing lab sample can stand in for the class); (2) health-function products carry implied duty to disclose PFAS regardless of affirmative safety claims; (3) public PFAS phase-out commitments demonstrate knowledge and undercut "reasonable consumer" defense; (4) fraud-based misrepresentation claims survive economic-loss rule where health-risk exposure is alleged. the elevation of internal RSL programs and phase-out targets as primary evidentiary documents — the stringency of a company's own PFAS thresholds can be used against it as the standard of care. For product stewardship work involving wearable tech, outdoor gear, medical device, and personal-care products, internal RSL programs and phase-out targets may interact with product-level PFAS concentrations in this evidentiary framework. Pinned as a foundational precedent. Source: National Law Review analysis.

The district court remanded to state court, finding the gravamen of the complaint concerned PFAS supplied to paper mills rather than federal conduct. On July 29, 2026, an Eleventh Circuit panel (Circuit Judge Charles R. Wilson, joined by Judges Newsom and Kidd) vacated the remand order, holding that the district court should have credited 3M's theory that the alleged contamination relates to PFAS contamination broadly rather than only to the paper-mill supply chain, and that Pine Hill's AFFF disclaimer functioned as artful pleading rather than a genuine narrowing of the claims. About this tracker & methodology

This tracker is a curated reference covering federal, state, international, litigation, and industry PFAS developments. It is produced by Library Services at Integral Consulting Inc. The resource is publicly accessible and free to share. It is built from publicly available sources, with the exception of InsideEPA articles, which require subscription access (Integral maintains a team subscription).

This page documents the inclusion criteria, editorial workflow, and AI use behind the tracker. Library Services maintains this resource with transparency about methodology as a core principle.

Inclusion criteria (curation gate)

Items are reviewed against a focused curation gate. Only developments with momentum qualify for inclusion:

  • Rule finalized; regulation entering force
  • Court ruling issued, especially at circuit or appellate level
  • Settlement reached or court-approved
  • Law signed
  • Major guidance or synthesis published
  • Quarterly or mid-year roundup from a credible source

Items that do not pass the gate: bills being drafted (until enacted or substantially advancing), speculative or rumor-stage coverage, courts considering stays, and industry groups weighing petitions. The goal is a dense, scan-friendly reference that holds up week to week.

Trending Now selection. The Trending Now section identifies topic areas with sustained underlying public activity within a rolling 60–90 day window. Items rotate off as activity slows; Library Services reviews the list quarterly as a backstop. Inclusion is observational, based on volume and recency of public developments documented elsewhere in this tracker, not a judgment about the merits of any party’s position or action. Each Trending item links to the underlying tracker entries for full context.

Editorial workflow (human-in-the-loop)

Every item on the tracker passes through a structured workflow:

  1. Scheduled scans. Four weekly scans run automatically: federal news (Fridays), state agency sweep across 19 states (Fridays), international sweep across EU/UK/Canada/Australia/Germany/Belgium/Brazil regulators (Fridays), and a Monday-morning Federal Register API check for any EPA, HHS/FDA, or DoD PFAS notices posted over the weekend.
  2. Source aggregation. Scans pull candidate items from trade press (InsideEPA, JD Supra, Lexology, National Law Review, law-firm blogs), state attorney general press releases, federal direct sources (Federal Register API, FDA pages, CPSC), and state agency landing pages.
  3. AI-assisted candidate identification. Each candidate is evaluated against the curation gate. AI assistance is used to sift the volume of public sources and produce structured candidate digests for human review. The AI does not render conclusions, characterize parties, or make recommendations.
  4. Human editorial review. The Library Services curator reviews every candidate, verifies the underlying source, applies the curation gate, and decides whether to include the item. Items that pass review are drafted into tracker entries.
  5. Neutrality discipline. Draft language is reviewed against an internal editorial guide that excludes value-laden verbs, administration-era prefixes, prescriptive language, and characterization of parties' conduct. Allegations are framed as allegations.
  6. Pre-publication hygiene check. An automated script verifies structural integrity, tab placement, filter counts, date accuracy, and section descriptions before any save.
  7. Manual final review. The curator does a manual editorial review of the rendered tracker before publishing.
  8. Publication. The updated tracker is pushed to GitHub Pages. The companion weekly digest is generated from the same content.

AI use disclosure

The tracker is AI-assisted. AI is used to:

  • Sift large volumes of public sources for candidate items
  • Draft candidate summaries from public source content for human review
  • Identify patterns across sources for the Trending Now section
  • Generate the structured weekly digest from approved tracker content

AI is not used to:

  • Render analytical conclusions or expert opinions
  • Characterize parties' conduct in litigation or regulatory matters
  • Make recommendations to readers
  • Process confidential client data of any kind

Inputs. The AI-assisted workflow operates on publicly accessible sources only: Federal Register notices, state agency publications, trade press, court records, published legislative texts, and agency guidance documents. Confidential or client-specific information is not entered into the tools used to support this tracker.

Every tracker entry is human-reviewed before publication. The "AI-Assisted, Human-Curated Tracker" label in the page header reflects this transparency.

Known limitations and bias mitigation

Both AI tools and the source materials that feed into this tracker carry known forms of bias. AI tools can reflect biases in their training data, produce plausible-sounding but inaccurate content, and over-weight recency or sensational framing. The trade press, law-firm blogs, and other published sources used as inputs also carry their own perspectives.

Integral Library Services applies the following editorial practices to counteract these biases:

  • Human review of every entry. No item is published without the curator reading the underlying source, verifying the facts, and rewriting any AI-drafted candidate text in neutral language. AI output is a starting point, not final content.
  • Cross-source verification. Material developments are confirmed against at least one primary source (Federal Register, court docket, agency publication, legislative text) before being added.
  • Editorial neutrality discipline. An internal style guide prohibits value-laden verbs, administration-era prefixes, prescriptive language to readers, and characterizations of parties’ conduct. The discipline is applied to defendants, plaintiffs, federal and state agencies, and administrators on the same standard.
  • Source attribution. Each card cites the underlying primary source so readers can evaluate the original material directly, rather than relying on the tracker’s summary.
  • Acknowledgment of source perspective. Where a source has an identifiable orientation (e.g., a law firm’s defense-side or plaintiff-side practice area), the tracker does not adopt that source’s framing in its own narration. Direct quotes are attributed; paraphrased material is rewritten neutrally.

These practices reduce but do not eliminate bias. Readers are encouraged to verify content against primary sources before relying on any tracker entry for professional analysis or deliverables.
